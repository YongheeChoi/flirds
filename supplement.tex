\section{Proofs and Formal Statements}
\label{app:proofs}

\subsection{System Model and Assumptions}
\label{app:model}

We assume synchronous FedAvg. All participants in $P_r$ begin local training
in round $r$ from the same model $w^r$ and submit model differences
$\delta_k^r$. The server aggregates them as
$w^{r+1} = w^r + \sum_{k \in P_r} p_k^r \delta_k^r$ with
$p_k^r = n_k / \sum_{j \in P_r} n_j$. The weights are held fixed
independently of the coalition $S$. The realized trajectory we fix is
$[(w^r, \{(\delta_k^r, n_k)\}_{k \in P_r})]_{r<R}$, and we write
$g_r = \nabla\lval(w^r)$, $H_r = \nabla^2\lval(w^r)$ (the true Hessian),
$\Delta_S^r = \sum_{k \in S \cap P_r} p_k^r \delta_k^r$, and
$\Delta W_r = \Delta_{P_r}^r$. Because $\phi$ attributes a decrease in
validation loss, a beneficial client receives $\phi_k > 0$.

The required system condition is not a stateless local optimizer but
stateless, additive server aggregation. When server state affects transitions
between rounds, as with server momentum or Adam, the grand-coalition
displacement no longer equals the realized update $w^{r+1} - w^r$, and the
telescoping argument below fails.

\subsection{The Round Game and Its Decomposition}
\label{app:game}

Define the round subgame and whole-run game as
$u_r(S) := \lval(w^r) - \lval(w^r + \Delta_S^r)$ and
$U^{\mathrm{in}} := \sum_r u_r$. The in-run Shapley is the Shapley value of
$U^{\mathrm{in}}$. Flirds computes exactly the Shapley value of
$\hat U^{\mathrm{in}}$, which is the sum of
$\hat u_r(S) := -\langle g_r, \Delta_S^r\rangle
- \tfrac12\langle \Delta_S^r, H_r \Delta_S^r\rangle$
(the first-order version is $\hat u_r^{(1)}$). The only difference between
the two games is the per-round Taylor remainder. We write $\hat\phi_k$ and
$\hat\phi_k^{(1)}$ for the Shapley values of $\hat U^{\mathrm{in}}$ and of
$\sum_r \hat u_r^{(1)}$, respectively.

The players are neither individual samples nor local optimization steps. Each
player is the weighted update $p_k^r \delta_k^r$ that one client contributes in
one round, and $\delta_k^r$ is itself the accumulation of that client's local
optimization steps. This game therefore
differs from the IRDS step game \citep{wang2025data}. Using the larger round displacement as the
expansion displacement affects the remainder analyzed in Supplementary
Section~\ref{app:remainder}. Moreover, the expansion point $w^r$ depends on the
realized trajectory, so the Shapley-axiom claims in the main paper apply only
to this fixed-trajectory in-run game. The retraining-based Shapley \citep{ghorbani2019data}
retrains from scratch
using only $S$, thereby changing both the weights and the trajectory. It is a
different game, and Section~5.2 of the main paper evaluates the relationship
between the two empirically.

\begin{lemma}[null-player reduction]
\label{lem:null}
If $v(\emptyset) = 0$ and $v(S) = v(S \cap T)$ for every $S$, then
$\phi_i(v) = 0$ for $i \notin T$ and $\phi_i(v) = \phi_i^T(v|_T)$ for
$i \in T$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every marginal contribution of $i \notin T$ is zero. The marginals of
$i \in T$ depend only on $S \cap T$. A uniform permutation of the full player
set induces a uniform relative order on $T$, so the resulting value equals
the permutation form of the Shapley value for the restricted game.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[round decomposition and efficiency]
\label{prop:decomp}
If $S \supseteq P_r$, then $u_r(S) = \lval(w^r) - \lval(w^{r+1})$, and thus
$U^{\mathrm{in}}([N]) = \lval(w^0) - \lval(w^R)$. By the Shapley efficiency
axiom, the values of all clients sum to the validation-loss decrease over the
complete run. Furthermore, $u_r(S) = u_r(S \cap P_r)$, so linearity and
Lemma~\ref{lem:null} give
\[
\phi_k(U^{\mathrm{in}}) = \sum_{r:\,k \in P_r} \phi_k^{P_r}(u_r|_{P_r}).
\]
\end{proposition}

This decomposition gives nonparticipating clients a value of zero in each
round. The decomposition itself does not require fixed weights. Fixed weights
are required for the coalition-independent additive structure and the closed
form below.

\subsection{Closed Form and Fixed Weights}
\label{app:closed}

\begin{theorem}[closed form]
\label{thm:closed}
If $\lval$ is twice differentiable at $w^r$ and $H_r$ is symmetric, then for
$k \in P_r$
\[
\phi_k(\hat u_r)
= -p_k^r \langle g_r, \delta_k^r\rangle
- \tfrac12 p_k^r \langle \delta_k^r, H_r \Delta W_r\rangle,
\]
which is Eq.~(6) of the main paper.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Writing $q_{ij} := p_i^r p_j^r\langle \delta_i^r, H_r\, \delta_j^r\rangle$,
with the fixed round index $r$ suppressed in $q$,
\[
\hat u_r(S)
= \sum_{k \in S}\Big(-p_k^r\langle g_r, \delta_k^r\rangle - \tfrac12 q_{kk}\Big)
- \sum_{\{i,j\} \subseteq S,\; i \ne j} q_{ij}.
\]
The first term is an additive game. By the Shapley symmetry and efficiency
axioms, each pairwise interaction in the second term is divided equally
between its two players. Hence,
$\phi_k(\hat u_r) = -p_k^r\langle g_r, \delta_k^r\rangle
- \tfrac12 \sum_{j \in P_r} q_{kj}
= -p_k^r\langle g_r, \delta_k^r\rangle - \tfrac12 p_k^r\langle \delta_k^r, H_r \Delta W_r\rangle$.
\end{proof}

In the final equality, every client shares the vector $H_r \Delta W_r$, so one
HVP per round suffices. The expression also holds when
$|P_r| = 1$, and $\sum_k \phi_k(\hat u_r) = \hat u_r(P_r)$ follows
immediately from the decomposition above.

Renormalizing the coefficients of the remaining clients within a partial
coalition gives
$\widetilde\Delta_S^r
= \sum_{k \in S} \frac{n_k}{\sum_{j \in S} n_j}\, \delta_k^r$,
which in general is not expressible as a sum of coalition-independent vectors
$b_k$. Even for two clients with equal $n$, the singletons force
$b_k = \delta_k^r$ while the pair gives
$\widetilde\Delta_{\{1,2\}}^r = \tfrac12(\delta_1^r + \delta_2^r) \ne b_1 + b_2$.
Fixed weights are therefore not required for every FL valuation, but they are
required for this additive structure and the one-HVP closed form. The
semantics also differ: ``had only these terms been reflected in the realized
round'' versus ``had only these clients participated.''

\textbf{Zero-update clients.} If $\delta_k^r = 0$, the estimator takes an
inner product with a zero tensor, so its value is algebraically exactly zero
regardless of numerical determinism. By contrast, an exact zero in the in-run
Shapley game presupposes bitwise determinism of the forward pass
(established for the CNN track, conditional for the LLM track). The
zero-update conclusions in Section~5.3 of the main paper and Supplementary
Section~\ref{app:intervention} rely on the estimator's algebraic zero.

\subsection{Taylor Remainder and Error Propagation}
\label{app:remainder}

Let $\mathcal U_r$ be an open convex set containing every coalition segment
$\{w^r + t\Delta_S^r : t \in [0,1],\, S \subseteq P_r\}$ and put
\[
M_{2,r} := \sup_{w \in \mathcal U_r}\|\nabla^2\lval(w)\|_{\mathrm{op}}, \quad
D_r := \max_{S \subseteq P_r}\|\Delta_S^r\|,
\]
\[
M_{3,r} := \sup_{w \in \mathcal U_r,\, \|x\| = 1}
|\nabla^3\lval(w)[x,x,x]|.
\]
The norms are the Euclidean norms of the relevant training coordinates.

\begin{proposition}[utility remainder]
\label{prop:remainder}
If $\lval \in C^3(\mathcal U_r)$ then
\[
\begin{aligned}
|u_r(S) - \hat u_r(S)| \le \tfrac{M_{3,r}}{6}\|\Delta_S^r\|^3, 
\\
\qquad |u_r(S) - \hat u_r^{(1)}(S)| \le \tfrac{M_{2,r}}{2}\|\Delta_S^r\|^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Apply the integral form of the Taylor remainder to
$h(t) = \lval(w^r + t\Delta_S^r)$ and bound
$|h''(t)| \le M_{2,r}\|\Delta_S^r\|^2$ and
$|h'''(t)| \le M_{3,r}\|\Delta_S^r\|^3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Shapley error propagation]
\label{lem:prop}
If $v(\emptyset) = v'(\emptyset) = 0$ then
$|\phi_i(v) - \phi_i(v')| \le 2\max_S |v(S) - v'(S)|$.
\end{lemma}

Each marginal contribution in the difference game is bounded by the
right-hand side, and a Shapley value is a convex combination of those
marginals.

\begin{corollary}[player-level bound]
\label{cor:player}
Applying Proposition~\ref{prop:remainder} and Lemma~\ref{lem:prop} to the
round decomposition of Proposition~\ref{prop:decomp},
\[
\begin{aligned}
|\phi_k(U^{\mathrm{in}}) - \hat\phi_k|
\le \sum_{r:\,k \in P_r} \tfrac{M_{3,r}}{3} D_r^3, 
\\
\qquad
|\phi_k(U^{\mathrm{in}}) - \hat\phi_k^{(1)}|
\le \sum_{r:\,k \in P_r} M_{2,r} D_r^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{corollary}

This bound establishes only the order of the remainder. Its constants are
difficult to estimate tightly for large models and accumulate across rounds.
Moreover, $D_r$ is the largest coalition displacement rather than the
realized aggregate displacement. The bound is conditional on the $C^3$
assumption and therefore applies to the LLM track with smooth activations
(Supplementary Section~\ref{app:lora}). For the ReLU-based CNN track, the
fidelity claim is supported only by the empirical results in Section~5.2 of
the main paper. Supplementary Section~\ref{app:remainder-measured} measures the
utility remainder of Proposition~\ref{prop:remainder} directly, by enumerating
the same coalitions on an LLM setting small enough to afford it.

\subsection{LoRA Coordinates}
\label{app:lora}

The game, estimator, and in-run Shapley for the LLM track are defined and
computed in the same LoRA-factor coordinates $\theta$. Therefore,
Supplementary Sections~\ref{app:game}--\ref{app:remainder} hold under the
substitution $\lval \to \tilde\ell_{\mathrm{val}}(\theta)$. The composition of
smooth activations with the polynomial map $\theta \mapsto W_0 + BA$ is
$C^3$, so the assumption of Supplementary Section~\ref{app:remainder} is
preserved as well.

The second-order surrogate is coordinate-dependent. Because $BA$ is bilinear,
the additive structure of factor updates generally cannot be reinterpreted as
per-client additive displacements in full-weight space. The curvature of the
LoRA map is itself part of the Hessian. Consequently, the closed-form and
remainder claims in this paper are limited to the LoRA-factor coordinates.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{app:protocol}

This appendix provides the details needed to rerun the experiments in
Section~5 of the main paper and reproduce the table entries. Section~5.1 and
the result-table captions already specify the model, dataset, partition,
number of clients, participation rate, and number of rounds for each setting,
and Section~5.1 states the corruption fraction once for all of them. Here, we
provide the remaining training and data
configuration. Supplementary Section~\ref{app:train} describes training,
Supplementary Section~\ref{app:conditions} details the conditions, and
Supplementary Section~\ref{app:data} specifies the data distribution and
evaluation sets.

\textbf{Table conventions.} The result tables in this supplement use the
following conventions, so that their captions can state the measured quantity
and the setting alone. An entry written as $a \pm b$ is the mean and standard
deviation over the three seeds $s \in \{0, 1, 2\}$, and tables abbreviate
Shapley value as SV. A mean row or mean column is the unweighted average of
the four displayed condition means and carries no standard deviation, matching
the main paper, and an em dash marks a quantity that is undefined for that arm
or condition. Where bold appears, it marks the best
value in that aggregate row or column, and the shaded row or column is Flirds.
A row or column labelled ``in-run SV'' reports the reference value rather than
a method and is excluded from that comparison, as are the vanilla,
oracle-exclusion, and selection-random arms. ComFedSV appears only in the
partial-participation settings, for the reason given in Supplementary
Section~\ref{app:train}.

\subsection{Training Configuration}
\label{app:train}

Both tracks use plain SGD without momentum and a constant learning rate:
$10^{-3}$ for the LLM track and $0.01$ for the CNN track. Per round, the LLM
track performs 10 local optimization steps, whereas the CNN track performs 5
local epochs with a batch size of 64. The LLM local batch size is reduced with model size
to fit memory: 16 at 1B, 8 at 3B, and 4 at 7B. The 1B and 3B models are the
instruction-tuned Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct
\citep{grattafiori2024llama}. The 7B
model is Llama-2-7b \citep{touvron2023llama}, a base model rather than an
instruction-tuned one, and
is the 7B model standard in the federated LLM literature
\citep{ye2024openfedllm}. The LLM track trains and
exchanges only LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} factors (rank 16, scaling factor 32) rather than the full
weights. The maximum sequence length is 512 in the main GSM8K and Alpaca
settings and 768 in the five-domain non-IID setting.
For CIFAR-10 \citep{krizhevsky2009learning}, we use FedSVCNN from the FedSV
line of work \citep{wang2020principled}: two
$5{\times}5$ convolutional layers with 32 and 64 channels, followed by a
512-unit dense layer. For MNIST, we use LeNet5 \citep{lecun1998gradient}. Two settings whose scales do
not appear in a result-table caption are the five-domain non-IID setting
($N{=}5$, full participation, $R{=}10$) and the Alpaca anchor ($N{=}5$, full
participation, $R{=}30$). All settings are trained and evaluated in fp32 with
PyTorch.

\textbf{Computing infrastructure.} The experiments were run on two Linux
machines: a node with NVIDIA B200 GPUs (AMD EPYC 9365, 2.2\,TiB of memory,
Ubuntu 24.04.3 LTS) and a node with NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs (Intel Xeon Gold
5320, 64\,GiB of memory, Rocky Linux release 9.4). Together they account for roughly 2{,}100
GPU-hours, about 1{,}180 of them on the RTX 3090 node and about 900 on the
B200 node. The software stack is Python 3.11 with PyTorch 2.11 (CUDA 13.0), transformers==5.5, PEFT 0.19, TRL 1.2, datasets 4.8.

\textbf{Baseline hyperparameters.} The remaining baseline settings are as
follows. GTG-Shapley \citep{liu2022gtg} draws permutations within a round
until the running mean over the last 10 of them changes by less than $5\%$,
evaluating at least $\max(30, |P_r|)$ and at most
$\lceil 0.8 \cdot 2^{|P_r|} \rceil$ permutations, and FedSV uses
$\max(30, 2|P_r|)$ permutations per round. For both methods we set the two
utility-difference truncation thresholds---within a permutation and between
rounds---to zero, so a coalition evaluation is skipped only by the convergence
criterion above, and the truncation constant $c$ of Table~4 in the main paper
is set by that criterion rather than by a utility threshold. ComFedSV draws
$M = \max(10, \lceil N \ln N \rceil)$ permutations and completes the observed
utility matrix by rank-5 alternating least squares (60 iterations, ridge
coefficient $0.1$) \citep{fan2022improving}. It is defined for partial
participation: under full participation every visited coalition is observed
directly and no entry requires completion, so we score it in the
partial-participation settings but not in the full-participation ones.
ShapleyFL \citep{sun2023shapleyfl} smooths its per-round contributions with an
exponential moving average of coefficient $\beta{=}0.3$, and FedIF
\citep{tang2025lightweight} smooths its round influences with an exponential
moving average of rate $\gamma{=}0.3$, the value its authors use for CIFAR-10.
We use both values in every setting.

\textbf{Hyperparameter selection.} The values above are fixed once per track
and used unchanged for every method, condition, model scale, and seed. No
hyperparameter is tuned per method or per condition, and no search over
learning rates, LoRA ranks, or round counts selects the numbers reported in
Section~5 of the main paper; each baseline keeps the hyperparameter values
published with it. Every method is therefore scored on the trajectory that
this single configuration produces, so a method's result reflects its
valuation rule rather than a separately tuned training setup.

\textbf{Seeds and determinism.} Every experiment reported in this paper is run
with the three seeds $s \in \{0, 1, 2\}$. A run is determined by that single
integer, which seeds Python, NumPy, and PyTorch at the start of the run and
therefore fixes the shuffling and partition of the training data, the sampling
of participants in each round, the drawing of the corrupted client set
(Supplementary Section~\ref{app:conditions}), the random streams that the
corruption condition uses,
and the Monte-Carlo permutations of the sampling-based baselines
(GTG-Shapley, FedSV, and ComFedSV). On the LLM track the LoRA
factors are initialized under a fixed seed before the run seed is applied, so
the initialization is identical across the three seeds.

\subsection{Implementation of the Conditions}
\label{app:conditions}

\textbf{clean} is the uncorrupted control for the four corruption
conditions below.

\textbf{answer-swap} (LLM) permutes the responses---including the derivation
and final answer---among a prescribed fraction of questions held by the same
client. The format remains valid, but the question--response correspondence
is broken, producing a realistic form of mislabeling. The fraction is $0.7$
in the main LLM setting and $1.0$ in the five-domain non-IID setting.

\textbf{zero-update free-rider} submits $\delta_k^r = 0$ without local
training.

\textbf{gradient noise} (CNN) adds i.i.d.\ Gaussian noise
$\mathcal{N}(0, 0.1^2)$ to every entry of the submitted update after normal
local training.

\textbf{label-flip} (CNN) replaces a fraction $0.7$ of a corrupted client's
labels with a label drawn uniformly from the other nine classes.

\textbf{Strengths and corrupted sets.} The corruption strength is a constant
in every setting and is never drawn from a distribution. Its value is stated
with each condition above. The corrupted set, by contrast, is realized
differently across settings, and we report the realized count rather than the
nominal fraction wherever the two can differ. In the main CNN setting
($N{=}100$), label-flip designates each client independently as corrupted with
probability $0.4$, so the realized count varies by seed: 39, 48, and 47
for seeds 0 through 2, a realized $44.7\%$ on average against the nominal
$40\%$. The two update-level conditions instead draw exactly
$40$ clients uniformly without replacement. The $N{=}10$ grid draws
exactly 4 clients under every corruption condition, so its three corruption
conditions share one corrupted set. On the LLM track the corrupted set is a
fixed index range:
clients $0$--$19$ of 50 in the main GSM8K setting ($40\%$), clients
$\{10, 30, 50, 70, 90\}$ of 100 in the cross-device setting ($5\%$), and one
of five clients in the five-domain non-IID setting ($20\%$). The Alpaca anchor
and the model-scale axis are clean throughout. A fixed index range does not
make the choice of corrupted clients special, because the GSM8K training set
is shuffled with the run seed before it is partitioned, so the same indices
hold different data in each seed.

\subsection{Data Distribution and Evaluation Sets}
\label{app:data}

For GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021training}, the 7{,}473 official training questions are distributed uniformly
and IID across clients, with 149 questions per client and the remainder left
unused. The server validation
set contains 200 of the 1{,}319 official test questions, and exact match is
measured on the remaining 1{,}119 questions. Training follows the official
split. Exact match is computed from greedy decoding without sampling, at most
256 new tokens, and prompts truncated to 512 tokens. In the five-domain non-IID
setting, each client holds one domain with 200 training, 20 validation, and 40
test examples. The five domains are medical
flashcards (Medical Meadow) \citep{han2023medalpaca}, legal QA
\citep{legalqa}, finance QA
(FiQA) \citep{maia201818}, math word problems with rationales (AQUA-RAT)
\citep{ling2017program}, and general instruction following (Dolly)
\citep{conover2023free}. We convert all five
public corpora to a common free-form
instruction--response format so that the shared validation loss is comparable
across domains. The cross-device setting, whose valuation cost Table~4 of the
main paper reports, draws on the same five domains but spreads them over
$N{=}100$ clients with a Dirichlet($\alpha{=}0.5$) mixture over domains per
client, so a client holds a skewed blend rather than a single domain. The
Alpaca setting partitions Alpaca-GPT4 \citep{peng2023instruction} 20k IID and holds out 200 validation
and 1{,}000 test examples. The anchor (Supplementary
Section~\ref{app:train}) and the model-scale axis of Figure~2 of the main
paper ($N{=}20$, 2/20 participation, $R{=}200$) differ only in client count,
participation rate, and round count.

The CNN track uses two partitions. The \texttt{iid} partition is a uniform
random split. In the \texttt{Dirichlet($\alpha{=}1$)} partition
\citep{hsu2019measuring}, label
distributions and client dataset sizes are jointly skewed. The run seed also
determines the partition. The $N{=}10$ grid uses 2{,}000 validation and
8{,}000 test examples. We report held-out test accuracy for the CNN track.

\subsection{Against the In-Run Shapley}

Table~\ref{tab:inrun-fidelity} collects both CNN comparisons that use the

and in both partitions the hardest condition is gradient noise ($\rho$ of

alone drops to $-0.188$ with the CIFAR-10 Dirichlet partition. The conclusion
from the partial-participation setting therefore also holds under full
participation.

\subsection{Value-Level Agreement}
\label{app:value-level}

Because Flirds and the in-run Shapley are values of the
same game in the same units, the \emph{magnitude} of the estimate can be
compared with the target directly as well, which adds information about the
target that the Spearman's $\rho$ and Pearson's $r$ do not carry.
Table~\ref{tab:value-level} reports that comparison in the $N{=}10$ grid,
where the in-run Shapley is enumerated exhaustively over $2^{10}$ subsets,
using two scale-sensitive quantities. Only Flirds and Flirds-1st are scored
there, because they alone produce values of the same game in the same units as
the reference. The relative $L_2$ error
$\|\hat\phi - \phi^{\mathrm{in}}\|_2 / \|\phi^{\mathrm{in}}\|_2$ is the
root-mean-square per-client error as a fraction of the target's own
root-mean-square size. Sign agreement is the fraction of clients on which the
two values agree in sign, which is the only property a sign gate reads.
Both are invariant to the global sign convention. Smaller is better for the
relative $L_2$ error and larger is better for sign agreement.

Three conditions behave as the correlations suggest and one does not. Under
the zero-update free-rider and label-flip, the relative $L_2$ error of
Flirds stays between $0.011$ and $0.109$. In the clean condition it grows to
$0.067$--$0.394$. Under gradient noise, however, it reaches
$0.408$--$0.730$ --- even though Table~\ref{tab:inrun-fidelity} reports
$\rho$ of $0.915$ and $0.968$ for the two CIFAR-10 cells. This is the regime
the bound of Supplementary Section~\ref{app:remainder} anticipates, since
injected noise inflates $\|\Delta_S^r\|$ and the remainder grows with its
cube. We therefore read the high correlations in that condition as evidence
about ranking alone.

The two variants separate here as well. Flirds holds sign agreement at
$100\%$ in 13 of the 16 cells and never falls below $86.7\%$, whereas
Flirds-1st drops to $60.0\%$ on both CIFAR-10 partitions under gradient
noise: on four of ten clients it reports the wrong sign, which is the
quantity an exclusion rule acts on.

\subsection{The LLM Retraining Leg}
\label{app:llm-fidelity}

We compute the retraining-based Shapley in two LLM settings
(Table~\ref{tab:llm-retrain}). The first is the
five-domain non-IID setting ($N{=}5$, full participation, $R{=}10$), where the
classical value is enumerated exhaustively through $2^5$ retraining runs from
scratch and compared with in-run enumeration for the corresponding realized
run. The two Shapley values have perfect measured correlations in all three
conditions (clean, answer-swap, and zero-update): Spearman's $\rho$ is
$1.000\pm0.000$, and Pearson's $r$ is $1.000\pm0.000$. Consequently, every
method's score against the retraining-based Shapley equals its score against
the in-run Shapley. Under that reference, GTG-Shapley, ShapleyFL, and both
Flirds variants reach $\rho = 1.000\pm0.000$ in all three conditions, FedSV
reaches $0.933\pm0.058$ under clean and zero-update and $1.000\pm0.000$ under
answer-swap, and FedIF ranges from $0.867\pm0.115$ to $0.933\pm0.058$. With
only five clients the ranking has little room to differ, so we do not read

\subsection{Direct Measurement of the Taylor Remainder}
\label{app:remainder-measured}

The rest of this appendix compares \emph{values}: an estimate against the
Shapley value it targets. This subsection instead measures the object that
separates them, the per-round Taylor remainder
$|u_r(S) - \hat u_r(S)|$ of Supplementary Section~\ref{app:remainder}.

\textbf{Protocol.} We use the five-domain non-IID setting
(Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, $N{=}5$, full participation, $R{=}10$, clean;
Supplementary Sections~\ref{app:train} and~\ref{app:data}). For every one of
the $2^5$ coalitions in each of the 10 rounds we
evaluate the realized round utility $u_r(S)$ of Eq.~(5) of the main paper and
its second-order surrogate $\hat u_r(S)$, and record the absolute difference.
The first-order remainder $|u_r(S) - \hat u_r^{(1)}(S)|$ is recorded on the

and it is no larger than the first-order
remainder in $80.1\%$ of the enumerated coalitions. The remainder is
therefore small in this setting relative to the quantity being attributed,
and retaining the curvature term shrinks it further.

\section{Reproducibility of the Target Signal}
\label{app:signal}

The clean condition behaves differently from the corrupted ones throughout
Section~5.2 of the main paper and Supplementary
Section~\ref{app:fidelity}: its correlations are lower and their spread across
seeds is markedly wider. This appendix locates the reason, which lies in the
reference value rather than in the estimators scored against it. A correlation
with a reference ranking transfers beyond the run it was measured in only to
the extent that the reference ranking is itself a stable property of the
clients \citep{geimer2025volatility}. When clients do not differ
systematically, that ranking changes from seed to seed.
Table~\ref{tab:target-stability} measures this directly, comparing the in-run
Shapley between seed pairs and reporting the mean and the minimum of
Spearman's $\rho$ over the three pairs. Larger values indicate a more
reproducible ranking. Where cross-seed agreement is low, a high within-run
correlation records the accurate reproduction of a ranking that is itself
unstable.

The measurement separates along the corruption and heterogeneity axes. In the
clean condition, the $N{=}5$ IID five-way split has cross-seed agreement of
$0.133$, whereas the five-domain non-IID split of the same size reaches
$0.867$. Domain heterogeneity alone therefore produces a reproducible signal.
Corruption acts on the same axis independently: the five-domain split rises
further to $0.933$ under both answer-swap and zero-update. The main GSM8K
setting follows the same pattern with
$N{=}50$ and 5/50 participation: its clean condition has agreement of
$-0.026$, while answer-swap and zero-update increase agreement to $+0.696$
and $+0.748$, respectively (Table~\ref{tab:target-stability}). These values
are computed over 50 clients per seed pair rather than five. Systematic client
differences, whether from heterogeneity or corruption, create the ranking
signal. The IID, clean condition has neither, so the order it presents is
close to seed noise. The wide per-seed spread of the clean cells records the
same fact.

Two results on the CNN track show the same mechanism from a different
direction. The CIFAR-10 IID clean cell of the $N{=}10$ grid starts from an
agreement between the two Shapley values of $-0.273$
(Table~\ref{tab:grid-retrain}): the in-run and retraining games agree closely
wherever clients differ systematically and drift apart where they do not. On
the same partition, the clean row of the removal curve puts every method near
zero, so the removal order does not change performance
(Table~\ref{tab:removal-cnn}). Both describe a setting in which client order
carries little information, rather than one in which the methods fail.

The attenuation is therefore a property of the setting rather than of any one
estimator. Within a cell, every method is scored against the same reference,
on the same realized trajectory, in the same seed, so the effect is common to
the whole column. We accordingly report and aggregate the clean condition on
the same footing as the others and read it as the control it is: it records
what the methods do when there is little to separate them, while the corrupted
and non-IID conditions supply the separation that the aggregate means reflect.
This also motivates the emphasis of the main settings, since a comparison is
most informative where the reference it is measured against is reproducible.

\subsection{The Online Point}
\label{app:online}

The first two panels of Table~\ref{tab:online} apply the gate online in the
same setting and with the same score sources as Section~5.3. The rule that decides exclusion is the one
used there---a client whose accumulated contribution is at most zero is
dropped---but it now runs during training and therefore needs three parameters,
which are shared by every score source and are never tuned per cell. Gating is
disabled for the first 10 rounds, a client is judged only once it has been
observed in at least 2 rounds, and every fifth round after that one excluded
client rotates back into the cohort, so that exclusion is not absorbing and a
client's score can recover. Contributions accumulate over rounds without
decay. The ordering from selection-retrain is
broadly preserved, but differences narrow because gating is disabled during
burn-in and the resulting model already reflects the corrupted updates from
that interval. Flirds alone recovers
under gradient noise ($56.68\%$ against $24.79\%$ for the first-order
family). The renormalizing family also remains below vanilla under the
zero-update free-rider ($39.15$--$40.20\%$ vs.\ $58.79\%$). Thus, the main
patterns are consistent across the two protocols.
The last panel of Table~\ref{tab:online} adds the IID selection-retrain
condition, whose Dirichlet counterpart appears in Table~2 of the main paper.

\subsection{The MNIST Repetition}
\label{app:mnist-rep}

Table~\ref{tab:mnist-int} repeats the same setting on MNIST. Outside the
gradient-noise column, all arms achieve between $92.7\%$ and $98.2\%$, so the
methods differ less than on CIFAR-10. Nevertheless, the differentiating
conditions show the same pattern: the first-order family remains at vanilla
performance under gradient noise, falling to $89.64\%$ in the Dirichlet
partition, and the renormalizing family falls below vanilla under the
zero-update free-rider.

\subsection{Set Accuracy of the LLM Gate}
\label{app:gate-sets}

Table~\ref{tab:gate-pr} evaluates the client set that the gate excludes in
Table~3 of the main paper rather than its downstream performance. Precision is
the fraction of excluded clients that are corrupted, and recall the fraction
of corrupted clients that are excluded. Neither variant excludes any client in
the clean condition, so both quantities are undefined there. Under the
zero-update free-rider, both variants exclude exactly the 20 corrupted clients
in every seed, which is why their EM in Table~3 matches oracle-exclusion.
The two corruption conditions differ in kind rather than in degree. Under
answer-swap, precision remains $100\%$, so the gate never removes a clean
client, but on average $5.0$ of the 20 corrupted clients survive for Flirds
and $6.7$ for Flirds-1st. The $1.5$-point gap to oracle-exclusion in Table~3 is
therefore entirely a recall gap, and the higher recall of Flirds is consistent
with its higher EM.

Applying the same gate online, as in Supplementary
Section~\ref{app:online}, separates the two corruption conditions much more
sharply. Scoring each (round, client) pair after the burn-in, where a
corrupted client counts as excluded in a round if the gate held it out of the
candidate pool or zeroed its weight, the online gate keeps precision high in
both conditions ($79\%$ and above), so the exclusions it makes are mostly
correct. The difference lies in coverage: it excludes the zero-update
free-rider in nearly every client-round, but reaches only a small fraction of
the answer-swap client-rounds, because the accumulated contribution of an
answer-swap client stays above the threshold for most of the trajectory. An
end-of-run gate can act on the evidence accumulated over the whole run,
whereas a per-round gate reading the running total largely cannot.

\subsection{Removal Curve}
\label{app:removal}

Table~\ref{tab:removal-cnn} evaluates the contribution ranking using
retraining performance outside the attribution game \citep{jia2019towards}. A wider gap between
removing clients from the lowest rank upward and removing them from the
highest rank downward indicates stronger alignment between the ranking and
performance. We report that gap averaged over all ten removal points. The
in-run Shapley and Flirds produce nearly identical gaps
under all three corruption conditions, differing by at most $0.15$~points.
Under gradient noise, only Flirds-1st reverses sign, reaching $-1.89$~points.
The zero-update free-rider divides the renormalizing family instead of
sinking it uniformly: ShapleyFL reverses sign at $-16.26$~points and FedSV
is unstable at
$+10.50\pm10.06$, whereas GTG-Shapley stays at $+21.68$, within $0.04$~points
of the in-run Shapley itself. This is narrower than the same family's uniform
failure under the intervention protocol of Section~5.3 of the main paper.
In the clean condition, every method is near zero, so removal
order has little effect on performance. This result is consistent with the
absence of a stable signal diagnosed in Supplementary
Section~\ref{app:signal}.

\section{Computational Cost}
\label{app:cost}

Section~5.4 of the main paper reports the cost of contribution evaluation in
two views, an analytic count of the dominant per-round operations and a
measured wall-clock time. This appendix supplies the per-operation constant
that links the two, reconciles them against each other, and states how the
ratio between methods changes with the number of participants per round.

\textbf{Per-operation microbenchmark.} Table~\ref{tab:microbench} times the two
operations that dominate the methods of Table~4 of the main paper, one
validation forward pass and one HVP along a fixed direction, on the same stack
that produced the wall-clock column of that table (fp32,
Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, one B200 GPU, the validation set processed in 10
chunks). One HVP costs $6.47$ validation forward passes. This single constant
is what makes one HVP per round comparable with the $2^{|P_r|}$ forward passes
per round of exhaustive enumeration, and it is the $6.5$ quoted in
Section~5.4 of the main paper.

The two operations were timed on a validation set of 100 examples, whereas the
cross-device setting whose runtimes Table~4 of the main paper reports uses 50
(Supplementary Section~\ref{app:data}). Both operations are linear in the
number of validation examples, so at 50 examples the absolute times halve to
$0.801$\,s for a forward pass and $5.180$\,s for an HVP while their ratio is
unchanged. The reconciliation below uses the 50-example values, and
Table~\ref{tab:microbench} must be rescaled the same way before it is
transferred to any other validation size.

\textbf{Reconciling the two views.} Table~\ref{tab:cost-check} multiplies each
method's dominant operation count by the corresponding 50-example constant and
compares the product with the measured total. Both predictions land within
$1.5\%$ of the measurement, so the operation counts and the wall-clock times
describe the same object rather than two loosely related ones. For Flirds the
agreement also bounds the side costs, because the prediction accounts for the
per-round HVP alone. The $|P_r|$ inner products and the storage of the round
updates together account for at most the remaining $1.2\%$ of the round, which
is why Table~4 of the main paper lists one HVP as the dominant operation.

Two independent routes therefore reach the same ratio between Flirds and
exhaustive enumeration. From the operation counts, the in-run Shapley needs
$2^{10}$ forward passes per round against one HVP for Flirds, giving
$2^{10}/6.47 = 158.3$. From the measurements alone, the Flirds total of
$157.3$\,s over 30 rounds is $5.24$\,s per round, which is $6.55$ forward
passes at 50 validation examples, and the two measured totals stand in the
ratio $24{,}975/157.3 = 158.8$. The $1/159$ of Section~5.4 of the main paper
is this last number, and the two routes agree to better than $1\%$, so the
figure does not depend on which view is used. Applying the same reconciliation
to Flirds-1st, whose dominant operation is one validation gradient, gives
$53.8/30 = 1.79$\,s per round. The cost of the second-order curvature term is
therefore the ratio of an HVP to a gradient, $5.180/1.79 = 2.9$, which is the
factor separating the two Flirds rows of Table~4 of the main paper.

\textbf{Dependence on the number of participants.} The Flirds cost is one HVP
per round independently of $|P_r|$, plus $|P_r|$ inner products that the
paragraph above bounds at about $1\%$ of a round, whereas exhaustive
enumeration of the round game costs $2^{|P_r|}$ forward passes. Combining the
two with the measured constant, the ratio grows as $2^{|P_r|}/6.47$: about $5$
at $|P_r|{=}5$, $158$ at $|P_r|{=}10$, and $5.1\times10^3$ at $|P_r|{=}15$.
Only the $|P_r|{=}10$ point is measured, and the other two are projections
from the operation counts and Table~\ref{tab:microbench} rather than separate
runs. The sampling-based baselines do not follow this curve, because their
permutation budgets are set by the convergence criteria of Supplementary
Section~\ref{app:train} rather than by $|P_r|$ alone.

\begin{abstract}
Data valuation quantifies how much of a trained model's performance is
attributable to each data provider, and the Shapley value, which satisfies the
axioms of fair allocation, is its standard tool. This question becomes most
pressing in federated learning (FL), where reward allocation, data markets,
and quality control all demand per-client contribution scores. Yet, constrained
by the conditions FL imposes, existing federated Shapley methods share two
limitations: exact computation of their target, the per-round
Shapley value, is exponential in the number of participants, so in practice
they resort to random-sampling and truncation approximations; and in the
process, each method redefines its own surrogate contribution measure, whose
deviation from the original Shapley value has been verified neither by
rigorous bounds nor against exact ground truths.
We propose \textbf{Flirds}, a client-level contribution valuation method that
answers these two limitations in turn. Flirds extends the closed-form
computation of In-Run Data Shapley (Data Shapley in One Training Run) to the
round structure of FL, computing per-round Shapley contributions from nothing
but the updates the server already receives, with no coalition re-evaluation.
The target per-round Shapley value is the Shapley value of an explicitly
defined round game, and therefore satisfies the axioms of fair allocation
within that game. Flirds approximates this target, but its sole
error, the Taylor truncation, is rigorously bounded; and it reproduces the
rankings of the ground truth obtained by computing the same target directly,
without approximation, via exhaustive enumeration (the exact in-run Shapley)
essentially perfectly at every scale of our 1B--7B LLM fine-tuning experiments
(Spearman $\rho \ge 0.999$). Going
further, in removal, selection, and detection experiments we assess whether the
measured contributions are genuinely valuable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\textbf{Data valuation, and federated learning.}
The quality of a modern machine-learning model is determined by its data. Data
valuation, which quantifies how much of a model's performance is owed to whose
data, has accordingly grown into a research axis of its own
\citep{ghorbani2019datashapley}, and the Shapley value from cooperative game
theory has become its standard tool: it is the unique allocation that
satisfies the axioms characterizing fair division (efficiency, symmetry, null
player, linearity) \citep{shapley1953}. The stage where this question is most
pressing is federated learning (FL) \citep{mcmahan2017fedavg}: contributors
are separated into clients from the outset; the server jointly trains a single
model without ever seeing their data; and the incentive structures that keep
clients participating, such as reward allocation, data markets, free-rider
prevention, and quality control and debugging, all hinge on the number that
says who contributed how much.

\textbf{Conditions of real FL settings.}
In deployed FL, contribution valuation must operate under the following
conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Information}: (1) The server cannot inspect any client's raw
    data individually: all it sees are the clients' model updates. (2) The
    validation data that anchors fair evaluation is held only by the server:
    clients' self-reports cannot serve as the standard.
    \item \textbf{Operation}: (3) Contributions must be computed online, every
    round: with clients participating intermittently (the cohort changes from
    round to round) and training continuing indefinitely, post-hoc batch
    evaluation is useless for settlement and operations. (4) Subset retraining
    is unaffordable, and valuation may impose neither additional communication
    rounds nor additional client-side computation. (5) The unit of valuation
    is the client, because clients are the subjects of reward and
    accountability.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Why not retrain-based Shapley values.}
The classical ground truth of data valuation is the retrain-based Shapley
value: the Shapley value of the utility obtained by retraining from scratch on
each subset of clients (Section~\ref{sec:game}). In FL, however, a different
set of clients participates intermittently in every round, and a fair
contribution assessment is required each time. In such an environment, this
definition, which presupposes subset retraining, is practically incomputable.
Existing federated Shapley studies have therefore mostly shifted the
evaluation target to the round level: compute the per-round Shapley value
defined on the updates the server receives in each round, and accumulate it
across rounds. This paper places its target at the same round level; that
target is what Section~\ref{sec:flirds} will define as the \emph{exact in-run
Shapley}.

\textbf{Two remaining limitations: bypassed exponential computation, and
unverified surrogate definitions.}
This round-level lineage \citep[FedSV, GTG-Shapley, ComFedSV, ShapleyFL, among
others;][]{wang2020fedsv,liu2022gtg,fan2022comfedsv,sun2023shapleyfl} is
mostly designed to operate within the conditions above. Two
limitations remain, however. The first is computation: computing the
targeted per-round Shapley value exactly requires reconstructing a model for
every client coalition in every round and re-evaluating it on validation data,
so the cost grows exponentially in the number of participants per round; in
practice this has been bypassed with random-sampling and truncation
approximations, and the entire lineage has remained at the scale of small-CNN
validation. The second is the unverified surrogate definitions that
this bypass produced: in the course of avoiding the cost, each method came to
target a different surrogate utility, some explicitly abandon the Shapley
axioms, and how far these surrogate values deviate from the original Shapley
value has been verified neither by rigorous bounds nor against exact ground
truths. The validity of the measured contributions has mostly been confirmed
only indirectly, through downstream evidence such as detection, convergence,
or final accuracy, and the cases that do score the estimates against exact
ground truths are limited to small synthetic settings or approximate
references. Reports that approximate FL-Shapley rewards swing by tens of
percent under a mere change of the server-side aggregation strategy
\citep{shapleyvolatility} are the practical face of this state of affairs. On
the opposite side, LLM-scale data valuation
\citep[In-Run Data Shapley (IRDS), DataInf, LESS, among
others;][]{wang2024irds,kwon2024datainf,xia2024less} has advanced rapidly, but
these methods are all centralized and sample-level, with direct access to the
data, which the FL constraints described above rule out.

Our approach targets exactly these two limitations. Extending the closed-form
Shapley computation that In-Run Data Shapley proposed for centralized training
to the round structure of federated
learning resolves both problems at once: coalition re-evaluation disappears
entirely, so the sampling and truncation used to bypass the exponential
computation are never needed in the first place (computation); and since both
the target and the approximation are explicit, the error can be rigorously
characterized, and the ground truth can be computed directly to score the
estimator (definition and verification).

\textbf{Proposed method.}
We propose \textbf{Flirds} (Federated Learning In-Run Data Shapley), a
client-level contribution valuation method that extends In-Run Data Shapley to
federated learning. The starting point is the observation that FedAvg-style
training already delivers to the server the ingredients that valuation needs:
every round, the server receives the participating clients' model updates and
aggregates them by a weighted average. Flirds views this per-round aggregation
as a cooperative game, asking how much the validation loss would have dropped
had only the updates of a subset of clients been applied, and computes the
exact Shapley value of this game in a closed form obtained by approximating
the validation loss with its first- plus second-order Taylor expansion.
Flirds, too, is thus an estimator that computes an approximation rather than
the ground truth itself. It differs from prior approximations, in which
sampling variance and unverified surrogate definitions pile on top of each
other, in that its approximation has a single source, the Taylor truncation,
and its error is bounded deterministically without any sampling
(Section~\ref{sec:flirds}). As a result, the heavy computation required per
round is fixed at a single
Hessian-vector product over the validation set, and the additional cost per
participant is one inner product: no retraining, no per-subset model
reconstruction and re-evaluation, and no extra communication or client-side
computation. Since the inner product scales only with the dimension of the
parameter space in which the updates live, the same formula applies unchanged
from small networks trained in full to LLM fine-tuning that exchanges only
PEFT (LoRA) adapters \citep{hu2022lora}.

\textbf{Verification.}
Prior studies have confirmed the validity of contribution scores mostly
through downstream effects: whether contribution-based selection helps
convergence or performance, or whether low-contribution clients turn out to be
corrupted. We split verification into two layers. In the estimator
layer, we directly compute, on the same training trajectory, the exact
Shapley value of the game Flirds targets, namely the exact in-run
Shapley computed from the in-run definition by exhaustively enumerating all
$2^{|P_r|}$ coalitions per round without approximation, and measure whether
the estimates reproduce it at both the ranking and the value level. This is a
stronger criterion than the prior practice of relying on approximate
references or indirect downstream evidence. In the effectiveness
layer, we measure whether the measured contributions are values that actually
help training. Each method's contributions are consumed to improve the
training process, and the methods are compared by the resulting training
outcomes: online sign-gating that automatically excludes clients with net
contribution $\le 0$ during training, removal experiments that remove clients
in contribution order and actually retrain to check the causal validity of the
ranking, selection experiments that retrain with only the top-contribution
clients, and detection of corrupted and free-riding clients. On top of this,
we measure every method's computational cost on the same fixed trajectory. The
empirical relationship to the classical exact retrain Shapley is reported only
as a characterization experiment (Section~5.3).

\textbf{Contributions.} The contributions of this paper are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Method.} We propose Flirds, a client-level contribution
    estimator that extends the closed-form computation of In-Run Data Shapley
    to the round structure of federated learning. To our knowledge, this paper
    is the first to perform federated client-level contribution valuation at
    LLM scale (PEFT fine-tuning).
    \item \textbf{Verification protocol.} Estimation accuracy is scored
    against the exact in-run Shapley computed by exhaustive enumeration, and
    the effectiveness of the contributions is scored by actual training
    improvement under an identical intervention policy. To our knowledge, we
    are the first to lift scoring against exact ground truths to LLM scale.
    \item \textbf{Mathematical justification.} We prove that the value Flirds
    computes is the exact Shapley value of the second-order Taylor
    approximation of the per-round aggregation game, and we bound the sole
    error, the Taylor truncation. The fairness axioms hold at the level of the
    round game, so a client that contributes nothing receives exactly zero
    algebraically; combined with round-level online settlement, these
    properties let the measured contributions be consumed directly as a basis
    for reward allocation over the realized run.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Shapley-based contribution valuation in federated learning.}
The lineage of federated Shapley values begins with FedSV
\citep{wang2020fedsv}, which decomposes client contribution into round-level
Shapley values and estimates them by permutation Monte Carlo sampling. The
flow that follows is a chain of approximations driving down the exponential
cost: GTG-Shapley \citep{liu2022gtg} uses within-round subset-model
reconstruction with guided-truncation Monte Carlo; ComFedSV
\citep{fan2022comfedsv} uses low-rank completion of a utility matrix to fill
in the coalitions left unobserved under partial participation; ShapleyFL
\citep{sun2023shapleyfl} uses per-round surrogate values processed with
normalization and moving averages; and the subsequent
FedIF--FedTSV--ShapFed--S-FedAvg family
\citep{fedif2025,fedtsv2026,shapfed2024,nagalapatti2021sfedavg} relaxes or
abandons the Shapley axioms and moves toward consuming the contribution signal
as robust-aggregation weights. In this lineage we observe precisely the two
limitations of Section~\ref{sec:intro}. The first is the bypass of
computation itself: all of the methods above substitute random sampling,
truncation, or interpolation for exact computation of the target Shapley
value, and their validation has likewise remained at small-CNN scale. The
second is the surrogate definitions and the verification gap the bypass
produced: in the course of cutting cost, different quantities (renormalized
games, interpolated loss matrices, processed surrogates) have come to be
estimated under the same name, yet their validity has mostly been confirmed
only indirectly through downstream results, and direct scoring against an
exact ground truth is confined to small scale, as in SPACE's $2^n$-retraining
comparison ($N \le 10$, CNN classification) \citep{space2024}.

\textbf{Centralized LLM-scale attribution.}
In centralized training, attribution and selection of individual training
examples have advanced actively up to LLM scale, along three strands. First,
the influence-function family estimates each example's effect on the
validation loss via gradients and the inverse Hessian ($H^{-1}$)
\citep{koh2017influence}, with LLM-scale variants differing in how they
approximate $H^{-1}$ (EK-FAC at 52B parameters \citep{grosse2023}, the
closed-form LoRA approximation of DataInf \citep{kwon2024datainf}, TRAK
\citep{park2023trak}, LoGra \citep{choe2024logra}). Second, the Hessian-free
wave of 2024 bypasses $H^{-1}$ altogether: LESS \citep{xia2024less} computes
TracIn-style trajectory influence \citep{pruthi2020tracin} with LoRA gradient
projections to select instruction-tuning examples, while MATES
\citep{yu2024mates} and DsDm \citep{engstrom2024dsdm} select pretraining data
with a distilled small model and a linear datamodel, respectively. Third,
In-Run Data Shapley (Section~\ref{sec:irds}) accumulates each step's Taylor
contribution along the actual training trajectory instead of using a post-hoc
$H^{-1}$. Among the three strands, it is the In-Run family that carries over
to the federated stage most naturally, because the FedAvg aggregation
$\sum_k p_k \Delta w_k$ already exposes, at the client level, the same
structure as the sample-linear decomposition of a batch gradient. This paper
starts from that observation and extends the closed-form computation of IRDS
to the federated round game (Section~\ref{sec:flirds}).

\textbf{Detection and robust-aggregation baselines.}
Federated learning has a separate lineage that counters corrupted or malicious
clients: detection, which identifies anomalous clients from update statistics
(FLDetector \citep{zhang2022fldetector}, FLTrust \citep{cao2021fltrust},
STD-DAGMM \citep{lin2019freerider}, FedDQC \citep{feddqc2024}, among others),
and robust aggregation, which secures robustness by suppressing the influence
of outlier updates at the aggregation step (Krum \citep{blanchard2017krum},
coordinate-wise median and trimmed mean \citep{yin2018byzantine}, among
others). These produce binary keep/discard decisions or trust weights; they do
not deal in signed continuous contributions with allocation axioms, and so are
not substitutes for contribution valuation. We keep the two axes separate and
compare against these dedicated detectors on the threats each was designed for
(Section~5.6).

\section{Background: Federated Learning, Data Shapley, and In-Run Data
Shapley}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Federated Learning and FedAvg}
\label{sec:fedavg}

In FedAvg \citep{mcmahan2017fedavg}, among clients
$k \in [N] := \{1, \dots, N\}$ holding local data of size $n_k$, the
participating set $P_r \subseteq [N]$ of round $r$ trains locally from the
current model $w^r$ and sends the difference as its update $\Delta w_k^r$; the
server aggregates for $R$ rounds starting from $w^0$ ($r = 0, \dots, R{-}1$)
with fixed participant weights proportional to data size:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fedavg}
w^{r+1} = w^r + \sum_{k \in P_r} p_k^r\, \Delta w_k^r,
\qquad
p_k^r = \frac{n_k}{\sum_{j \in P_r} n_j}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Data Shapley}
\label{sec:datashapley}

In a cooperative game whose utility function $U$ assigns value to coalitions
$S$, the \emph{Shapley value} averages player $k$'s marginal contribution over
all participation orders,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shapley}
\phi_k(U) = \sum_{S \subseteq [N] \setminus \{k\}}
\frac{|S|!\,(N{-}|S|{-}1)!}{N!}\, \big( U(S \cup \{k\}) - U(S) \big)
\end{equation}
(the same formula for any finite player set), and is the unique allocation
satisfying the four axioms of fair division---efficiency
($\sum_k \phi_k(U) = U([N]) - U(\emptyset)$), symmetry, null player, and
linearity \citep{shapley1953}.
Data Shapley \citep{ghorbani2019datashapley} applies this to data
contribution by treating the training data as the players and setting $U(S)$
to the validation performance of a model trained on the data of $S$ alone.

\subsection{In-Run Data Shapley}
\label{sec:irds}

In-Run Data Shapley (IRDS) \citep{wang2024irds} fixes the one training run
that actually happened, in place of hypothetical retrainings, and views every
gradient step as a small cooperative game. When the SGD update at step $t$ is
$w^{t+1} = w^t - \eta_t \sum_{z \in B_t} \nabla \ell_z(w^t)$, where $B_t$ is
the batch, $\ell_z$ is the training loss of sample $z$, and $\lval$ is the
mean loss on the validation set, the utility of $S \subseteq B_t$ is the
validation-loss decrease obtained by applying only the gradients of $S$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:irds-utility}
u_t(S) = \lval(w^t)
- \lval\Big(w^t - \eta_t \textstyle\sum_{z \in S} \nabla \ell_z(w^t)\Big),
\end{equation}
and values are accumulated as $\phi_z = \sum_{t:\, z \in B_t} \phi_z(u_t)$
(notation: $\phi_z(u_t)$ is the Shapley value of the game $u_t$ via
Eq.~\eqref{eq:shapley}; the argument-free $\phi_z$ is its accumulation). The
key observation: Taylor-expanding $u_t$ at $w^t$ yields the Shapley value of
the approximate game in closed form. Writing
$g_t := \nabla \lval(w^t)$ and $H_t := \nabla^2 \lval(w^t)$ for the gradient
and Hessian of the validation loss, the first-order game is additive, so its
Shapley value is the gradient inner product itself; taking the expansion to
second order,
\begin{multline}\label{eq:irds-closed}
\phi_z(u_t) \approx \eta_t \big\langle g_t,\, \nabla \ell_z(w^t) \big\rangle \\
- \tfrac{\eta_t^2}{2} \Big\langle \nabla \ell_z(w^t),\;
H_t\, \textstyle\sum_{z' \in B_t} \nabla \ell_{z'}(w^t) \Big\rangle
\end{multline}
(signs follow the loss-decrease convention of Eq.~\eqref{eq:irds-utility}).
The Shapley axioms are inherited at the level of the step games; efficiency
combines with telescoping to give
$\sum_z \phi_z = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} u_t(B_t) = \lval(w^0) - \lval(w^T)$, where
$T$ is the total number of steps; and a sample with zero gradient is a null
player receiving exactly zero (see the original paper for formal statements
and proofs). The question it asks differs from the retrain definition
(counterfactual versus realized attribution), and its values are
trajectory-specific.

\section{Flirds: The Federated In-Run Game and Its Closed-Form Estimation}
\label{sec:flirds}

\subsection{The Federated Game and the Two Ground Truths}
\label{sec:game}

The classical ground truth of data valuation, the \emph{exact retrain
Shapley}, retrains from scratch for $R$ rounds with only the client subset $S$
participating and takes the validation-loss decrease of the resulting final
model $w_S^R$ (with $w_\emptyset^R = w^0$),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:retrain}
U^{\mathrm{re}}(S) = \lval(w^0) - \lval\big(w_S^R\big),
\end{equation}
whose Shapley value $\phi_k^{\mathrm{re}} = \phi_k(U^{\mathrm{re}})$ is
computed without approximation by retraining all $2^N$ subsets (a
method-neutral ground truth; the question it asks is the counterfactual ``had
only $S$ participated''). The round structure of FL, meanwhile, corresponds
exactly to the step structure of IRDS: in the step game of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:irds-utility}, substituting the round's weighted per-client
update $p_k^r \Delta w_k^r$ for the sample gradient term
$-\eta_t \nabla \ell_z$ transplants the game to the client level, and the
ingredients are nothing but what the server receives anyway under the standard
protocol. We freeze the realized trajectory
$\{w^r, \{\Delta w_k^r\}_{k \in P_r}\}_{r=0}^{R-1}$ (the \emph{frozen
trajectory}; every method below consumes this same training log) and define
the per-round coalition utility, for $S \subseteq P_r$, as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:round-utility}
u_r(S) = \lval(w^r)
- \lval\Big(w^r + \textstyle\sum_{k \in S} p_k^r\, \Delta w_k^r\Big).
\end{equation}
The weights are the \emph{fixed weights} $p_k^r$ that the run itself used; we
do not renormalize within $S$. Renormalizing within $S$, as some baselines do
(GTG-Shapley, FedSV), is not an implementation detail but a \emph{different
cooperative game}: a zero-update free-rider comes to receive a nonzero value;
there are counterexamples in which rankings flip under unequal data sizes,
partial participation, and second-order curvature (Appendix~A); and the
sum-form structure that folds a round into a single HVP breaks, so the
closed-form computation no longer holds. The \emph{exact in-run Shapley}
$\phi^{\mathrm{in}}$ is the sum of each round game's Shapley value computed
without approximation by exhaustively enumerating all $2^{|P_r|}$ subsets per
round: $\phi_k^{\mathrm{in}} = \sum_{r:\, k \in P_r} \phi_k(u_r)$. The
efficiency of IRDS carries over, combined with telescoping:
$\sum_k \phi_k^{\mathrm{in}} = \sum_{r=0}^{R-1} u_r(P_r)
= \lval(w^0) - \lval(w^R)$.

\textbf{Terminology.} Throughout, ground truth (oracle) refers to exactly two
objects: the \emph{exact retrain Shapley} above (exhaustive per-subset
retraining) and the \emph{exact in-run Shapley} just defined (exhaustive
per-round enumeration). We always use the full names and never an unqualified
``exact.'' For both, larger is more beneficial (positive when the validation
loss is reduced).

\subsection{The Closed-Form Estimator}
\label{sec:estimator}

The exact in-run Shapley involves no approximation, but it demands
$2^{|P_r|}$ validation evaluations per round. Flirds starts from the fact that
the Shapley value of $\hat u_r$, the approximate game obtained by
Taylor-expanding the round utility to second order around $w^r$, comes out in
closed form (Proposition~\ref{prop:closed}). Writing
$g_r := \nabla \lval(w^r)$, $H_r := \nabla^2 \lval(w^r)$ (the true Hessian,
not a Gauss--Newton approximation), $\delta_k^r := \Delta w_k^r$, the
aggregate displacement of a coalition
$\Delta_S^r := \sum_{k \in S} p_k^r\, \delta_k^r$, and
$\Delta W_r := \Delta_{P_r}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:closed}
\begin{split}
\hat\phi_k^{(r)}
&= -\,p_k^r\, \big\langle g_r,\, \delta_k^r \big\rangle
- \tfrac{1}{2}\, p_k^r\, \big\langle \delta_k^r,\; H_r\, \Delta W_r
\big\rangle, \\
\hat\phi_k &= \sum_{r\,:\,k\in P_r} \hat\phi_k^{(r)}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The first term measures how well $k$'s update aligns with the descent
direction of the validation loss; the second, the \emph{client-interaction
term}, measures how $k$'s update composes, through curvature, with the other
participants' aggregate update. (The sign of the first term differs from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:irds-closed} because there the displacement
$-\eta_t \nabla \ell_z$ carries an explicit minus sign, whereas here
$\delta_k^r$ is itself the displacement.) All a round requires is \emph{one}
Hessian-vector product (HVP) $H_r \Delta W_r$, computed with forward-mode
automatic differentiation, plus one inner product per participant. No Hessian
is ever materialized or inverted, and the cost is independent of the number of
subsets $2^{|P_r|}$. The variant with the second-order term removed is called
\emph{Flirds (first-order)} and serves as an ablation of the interaction term.

\subsection{Theory: The Estimator and the Exact In-Run Shapley Compute the
Same Game}
\label{sec:theory}

The point of this section is one sentence: Flirds computes not an arbitrary
score but the Shapley value of precisely the game defined by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:round-utility}, and its sole difference from the exact in-run
Shapley is the Taylor truncation. Formal statements, proofs, assumptions,
counterexamples, and numerical verification are in Appendix~A.

\begin{proposition}[Per-round decomposition]\label{prop:decomp}
On the frozen trajectory, the Shapley value of the total game $\sum_r u_r$
(each $u_r$ extended to a game on $[N]$ by $u_r(S) := u_r(S \cap P_r)$ before
summing) decomposes exactly into the sum of the Shapley values of the
per-round $|P_r|$-player games. (That non-participating rounds contribute
zero is a theorem, not a definition.)
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Closed form; free-rider zero; efficiency]
\label{prop:closed}
For the second-order Taylor approximation game $\hat u_r$ of the round
utility, the exact Shapley value equals Eq.~\eqref{eq:closed} exactly:
$\hat\phi_k^{(r)} = \phi_k(\hat u_r)$ (proof in Appendix~A). Two corollaries:
a zero-update free-rider with $\delta_k^r = 0$ in every participating round
receives \emph{exactly} $\hat\phi_k = 0$ algebraically, and the values sum to
the total utility of the approximate game (efficiency).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Taylor residual]\label{prop:residual}
The price of the approximation is truncation error. The per-round utility
error is bounded by $\frac{M_3^r}{6}\|\Delta_S^r\|^3$ at second order and by
$\frac{M_2^r}{2}\|\Delta_S^r\|^2$ at first order, and accumulates linearly in
the number of rounds (the per-round local curvature constants
$M_2^r, M_3^r$ are defined formally in Appendix~A). These constants are not
estimable quantities, so what the bound delivers is the \emph{order} of the
residual (its scaling law).
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[Scope]
The exact in-run Shapley is a function of the realized trajectory, and
therefore inherits IRDS's unresolved axiomatization of trajectory-specific
values (its relation to the exact retrain Shapley is an empirical question
rather than a theoretical one, and Section~5.3 reports it as such). The game
is defined at \emph{client} granularity, the unit the FL protocol exposes to
the server; the formal per-sample-to-per-client bridge holds under mean losses
and a single local step (Appendix~A) and does not transfer as-is to token-mean
LLM losses. In the LLM track, $w$, $\delta_k^r$, $g_r$, and $H_r$ are all
quantities in the LoRA-factor coordinates that the server actually exchanges
and averages (the CNN track trains full parameters, so no distinction arises;
the formal treatment of coordinate dependence is in Appendix~A.10).
\end{remark}

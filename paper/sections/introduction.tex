\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Federated learning trains a shared model from clients whose data the server never
sees \citep{mcmahan2017fedavg}. Knowing \emph{how much each client contributed} is
the entry ticket to incentive design, debugging, data markets, and quality
control---and a long line of work therefore assigns clients Shapley-style values
\citep{wang2020fedsv, liu2022gtg, fan2022comfedsv, sun2023shapleyfl}. Because
retraining over client subsets is unaffordable, essentially all practical methods
are \emph{in-run}: they score clients from quantities produced by the single
training run itself (per-round updates, gradients, or reconstructed sub-models).

This creates a validation dilemma that the field has mostly resolved indirectly.
An in-run score has no free-standing ground truth, so prior work argues its
validity through \emph{consequences}: valuation-weighted aggregation converges
faster, low-value clients turn out to be corrupted, selected clients yield better
models \citep{sun2023shapleyfl, fedif2025, shapfed2024}. Fidelity to the
contribution game itself---``does the score equal the Shapley value of anything
well-defined?''---is evaluated rarely; where it is measured against exact
ground truth at all, it is at CNN scale, with small $N$, and against a single
oracle definition \citep{liu2022gtg, space2024}, with approximate references
elsewhere \citep{fan2022comfedsv}; at LLM scale, to our knowledge, never
against an exact oracle. Indirect validation is also fragile in its own right:
downstream effects conflate the score with the intervention rule, and
Shapley-based rewards in FL are volatile under seemingly innocuous protocol
changes \citep{shapleyvolatility}.

\textbf{This paper takes the measurement-first route.} We fix the trajectory
that FedAvg actually produced and define \emph{two} exact ground truths on it:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{(a) Retrain oracle} $\aOracle$: the classical Shapley value with
utility ``final validation loss of FL training restricted to subset $S$,''
computed exactly by $2^N$ retrains. This is the data-valuation community's
standard, method-neutral target.
\item \textbf{(b) In-run oracle} $\bOracle$: the exact Shapley value of the
per-round aggregation game---the validation-loss change produced by aggregating
exactly the updates of subset $S$ at each round---computed by exact $2^{|P_r|}$
coalition enumeration per round on the frozen trajectory. This is the formal
object that in-run methods approximate, made explicit and computed without
sampling error.
\end{enumerate}
Both oracles are expensive (the retrain oracle took $8.6$ GPU-hours per seed
for a single $N{=}5$, 1B-parameter cell in our runs \verify{$30{,}817\pm244$s,
track\_d 1B anchor, file-canon}), which is precisely why they are absent from
prior evaluations---and why an estimator that tracks them cheaply is useful.

\textbf{Our estimator.} \textsc{Flirds} (Federated Learning In-Run Data Shapley)
extends In-Run Data Shapley \citep{wang2024irds} from centralized per-step,
per-sample attribution to federated per-round, per-client attribution. It computes
a closed-form first- plus second-order Taylor expansion of the in-run game around
the current iterate, using only the client updates $\{\Delta w_k\}$ (LoRA
parameters) that the server already receives: one Hessian--vector product and
$|P_r|$ inner products per round, zero extra communication, and cost independent
of the per-round cohort size---in contrast to coalition-enumerating baselines whose cost
grows as $2^{|P_r|}$ per round.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em, itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{A measurement standard.} An evaluation of federated,
client-level valuation at LLM scale against \emph{exact} $2^N$ dual
oracles---a combination our systematic taxonomy of $\sim$40 prior methods
(\S\ref{sec:related}) finds unoccupied; we state this as an \emph{observed
gap}, not a novelty claim. The protocol enforces fp32 discipline (utility
differences of order $10^{-3}$ are below bf16 resolution,
\S\ref{sec:protocol}), fixed frozen trajectories shared by all methods,
multi-seed reporting, and value-level as well as rank-level fidelity metrics.
\pending{D-15 wording strength}
\item \textbf{A cheap, faithful estimator.} \textsc{Flirds} reproduces the exact
in-run oracle ranking across 1B/3B/7B and across IID, non-IID, and
partial-participation stages (Spearman $0.967$--$1.000$; exactly $1.000$ in all
LLM cells but one), at $5$--$160\times$ lower cost than exact enumeration for
cohorts of five or more; at cohort two, exact enumeration is cheaper and we say
so (\S\ref{sec:results-cost})---the advantage is decisive precisely where exact
valuation is infeasible.
\item \textbf{Theory aligning estimator and oracle.} We prove that the estimator
and the in-run oracle value \emph{the same fixed-weight cooperative game}, the
sole difference being second-order Taylor truncation (\S\ref{sec:theory}); we give
the per-round decomposition, closed form, residual bounds, and the exact
conditions under which the fixed-weight game and the subset-renormalized game
used implicitly by prior baselines agree or provably diverge.
\item \textbf{A boundary map.} Measurement-first evaluation reveals structure
that indirect validation cannot: (i) on IID-clean stages the game is
near-additive and carries \emph{no} cross-seed ranking signal---the correct
intervention answer there is ``do no harm,'' and no method (including the exact
oracle) can demonstrate downstream gains; (ii) genuine inter-client differences
(domain heterogeneity, corruption, participation asymmetry) create the signal,
and there the completion- and surrogate-based baselines collapse while
\textsc{Flirds} remains faithful;
(iii) clean-loss-preserving backdoors evade first-order valuation by
construction, and the second-order term partially restores detection---a
principled, scale-dependent failure boundary we characterize rather than hide.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{abstract}
Client-level data valuation in federated learning (FL) is almost universally
justified indirectly: a per-round, in-run contribution score is proposed, and its
validity is argued through downstream effects such as convergence speed, robust
aggregation, or corrupted-client detection. Whether the score itself faithfully
measures the contribution game it claims to measure is rarely tested, and---at LLM
scale---has, to our knowledge, never been tested against an exact Shapley oracle.
We take the opposite route: \emph{measure first}. We define a dual ground truth for
client-level valuation on a fixed FedAvg trajectory---(a) the exact retrain Shapley
value over all $2^N$ client subsets, and (b) the exact in-run Shapley value of the
per-round aggregation game---and evaluate ten valuation methods against both, on
LoRA-fine-tuned LLMs (1B/3B/7B) and CNN control tracks, under IID, non-IID,
partial-participation, and adversarial regimes. Our estimator, \textsc{Flirds},
computes a closed-form first- plus second-order Taylor approximation of the
in-run game from the updates the server already receives, at one
Hessian-vector product per round and zero additional communication; it reproduces the
exact in-run oracle ranking (Spearman $\rho \ge 0.999$ across scales) at a cost
independent of the per-round cohort size, where exact coalition enumeration is up to
$160\times$ more expensive. The measurement-first protocol also yields a boundary
map that indirect validation cannot see: on IID-clean stages the contribution game
is near-additive (non-additivity $\le 0.9\%$) and its own cross-seed ranking signal
is statistically null, so \emph{no} method can demonstrate downstream utility there;
real signal appears only with genuine inter-client differences (heterogeneity,
corruption, partial participation), exactly where popular baselines collapse
(e.g., Spearman $+1.00$ for \textsc{Flirds} vs.\ negative correlations for
ComFedSV/ShapleyFL at $5/50$ participation); and clean-loss-preserving backdoors
constitute a principled failure mode of first-order valuation that the second-order
